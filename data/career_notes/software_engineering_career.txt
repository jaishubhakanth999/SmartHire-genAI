Software Engineering Career Guide

Software engineering roles vary widely by specialization: backend, frontend, full-stack,
DevOps/SRE, mobile, embedded, and machine learning engineering are among the most common.
Most roles share a foundation: comfort with at least one general-purpose language (Python,
Java, Go, JavaScript, C++ are the most in-demand), data structures and algorithms, and
version control with Git.

Getting started
If you are early in your career, build two or three complete projects you can walk a
hiring manager through end to end -- a project with a real user, a database, an API, and
deployed somewhere (even a free tier) shows more than a collection of isolated scripts.
Contribute to an open-source project, even in a small way (documentation fixes, tests,
minor bugs), to show you can navigate an unfamiliar codebase.

Technical interviews
Most software engineering interview processes include:
1. A phone or video screen with a few coding questions (linked lists, strings, hashmaps,
   basic trees -- LeetCode Easy/Medium level).
2. A longer take-home or live coding round, often with a real-world design element.
3. A system design round (for mid-to-senior roles): how would you design a URL shortener,
   a notification system, a feed -- covering scalability, data modeling, trade-offs.

Resume conventions
Use bullet points that follow the "did X, measured by Y, to achieve Z" structure.
Quantify impact where possible: lines of code shipped rarely impresses but "reduced API
latency by 40%" or "cut deploy time from 30 min to 5 min" does.
Keep the resume to one page for under five years of experience; two pages is acceptable
beyond that.

Salary negotiation
Software engineers frequently under-negotiate. Research market rates on Glassdoor, Levels.fyi
(for tech companies), and LinkedIn Salary before any offer call. It is standard practice
to ask for time to consider and to negotiate; almost no company rescinds an offer because
a candidate asked for more.
